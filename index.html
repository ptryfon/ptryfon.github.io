    How the force is made and handed to the arrow

    1. The limbs are the spring

      Every bit of energy you put in ends up as bending in the limbs. Pulling the string turns the cams, the cams wind in the cables, and each cable pulls the opposite limb tip toward the middle. The limbs are already bent at rest (preloaded by the limb bolts), which is why the string is tight before you touch it.

    2. The cams are levers with a changing ratio

      Each cam has two tracks: the string track pays out string, the cable track (module) winds up cable. Around the axle the torques balance, so string tension × string arm = cable tension × cable arm.

      Early in the draw the cable arm is long compared with the string arm, so most of the limb load comes straight through to your hand and the weight climbs fast. Near full draw the cable track swings in close to the axle while the string arm grows. The limbs are bent the most, but the lever now multiplies your effort 20 to 50 times. That is let-off.

    3. Energy is the area under the curve

      Stored energy is force × distance, added up across the draw. A compound jumps to peak weight early and stays there, so the curve is nearly a rectangle. A recurve only reaches the same peak at full draw, a triangle, and stores far less. The compound also lets you hold a fraction of that weight while you aim.

    4. Handing it to the arrow

      Let go and the limbs unbend, haul the cables back, spin the cams in reverse and drive the string forward. The force on the nock replays the curve backwards: a gentle start from the valley, a surge to full peak weight as the cams roll back over, then a fade to zero at brace height, where the arrow separates. The whole push lasts less than 30 milliseconds and can peak above a thousand g.

      Not all of it leaves with the arrow. The limbs, cams and string have to be accelerated too, so they keep some of the energy, and a little turns into heat inside the limbs. A heavier arrow takes a bigger share: more efficient, but slower. What stays behind rattles the bow as vibration and noise, which is what dampers soak up, and why a dry fire with no arrow to take the energy can wreck a bow.

    Model: a generic dual-cam bow, 33" axle to axle, 6.5" brace height.
